The bet

Most first field events fail the same way

A young company books a venue, buys some ads, puts its own executives on stage, and then wonders why the room is half full of people who will never buy. The event becomes a cost center that produces a photo and nothing else.

When I built the first San Francisco field program at Aiseberg, the company behind Aisepedia, I started from the opposite assumption. The room is the product. If the right 30 people are in it, the content almost does not matter. If the wrong 200 are in it, the best content in the world is wasted. So the entire job was getting the room right, and everything else followed from that.

The premise of the event was a point of view, not a pitch. We called it PMM 2.0: Redefining the role for maximum impact. The claim was that product marketing is changing shape, and the people who feel that most acutely are senior product marketers themselves. That gave me a reason to gather them that had nothing to do with selling anything. A good field event earns attention by being about the audience's problem, not the host's product.

Sourcing the panel

Through the network, not the checkbook

I did not buy a single speaker. I did not run paid promotion. Both were deliberate.

The panel came through the design partner network I had already built, a 27-member cohort with 18 people at Director, VP, or SVP level. When you have spent months in real conversations with senior operators, asking one of them to sit on a stage about the future of their own craft is a natural next step, not a cold ask. The four panelists came from Atlassian, Meta Reality Labs, Google, and a background spanning Celona, Google, and Zscaler. Every one of them was reachable because the relationship already existed.

That is the part most people skip. The event was not the start of the relationship. It was a payoff of relationships built earlier. A field program is a lagging indicator of your network, and you cannot shortcut it with a budget.

Owning it end to end

One person holding every thread

There was no events team. I owned the venue, the speakers, the run of show, the promotion, and the follow-up. That sounds like a burden and it is actually the advantage. When one person holds every thread, the event has a single point of view and nothing falls between departments. The invitation, the framing on stage, and the follow-up conversation all said the same thing, because the same person wrote all three.

The promotion was entirely earned. No ads. The draw was the premise and the panel, carried through direct outreach and the credibility of the people on stage. If the topic and the room are right, you do not need to pay for attention. If you need to pay for attention, the topic and the room are usually wrong.

What happened

A room of exactly the right people

Thirty-six senior product marketers filled the room, drawn without any paid promotion. The four panelists carried the conversation about where the role is heading, in front of exactly the audience who lives that question. The follow-up moved attendees into product conversations and, for some, into the design partner program itself.

I will be precise about what I am claiming, because a number only matters when it says what it does and does not mean. The attendance and the panel are verified. I am not attributing a specific pipeline figure to the event here, because that would overstate a clean line that does not exist. What the event produced was a room of the right people, a public stage for the partner network, and a set of warm follow-ups. That is what a first field program is supposed to do.

The lessons

What I would tell another GTM and product growth operator

Build the network before you need the room. The event was easy to fill because the hard work happened in the months before it, in discovery calls and partner conversations that had no event attached. If you are trying to source a panel from strangers two weeks out, you started too late.

Make the event about the audience's problem, not your product. Nobody senior gives up an evening to watch a vendor pitch. They will give up an evening to argue about the future of their own work with peers they respect. Your product shows up in the follow-up, not on the stage.

Own it end to end, at least the first time. The coherence you get from one person holding every thread is worth more than the efficiency you would get from splitting it up. You can systematize it later. The first one teaches you what the system needs to be.

A field program is not a marketing tactic you bolt onto a GTM motion. Done right, it is the GTM motion made visible for one evening: the positioning, the network, and the follow-up, all in one room.
